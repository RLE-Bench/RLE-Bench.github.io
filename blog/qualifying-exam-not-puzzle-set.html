Design

A qualifying exam,
not a puzzle set

Eight families of the work a robot learning engineer actually does. Here is the brief we wrote for ourselves before writing any of them, the filter that killed most candidate tasks, and why the index is an unweighted mean rather than something cleverer.

The question we wanted to answer was narrow and, we thought, answerable: could you hire this agent as a robot learning engineer? Not can it write a sort function, not can it recall the equations of a manipulator Jacobian — could it be handed a week of the actual job and come back with something that works.

That framing did more work than any individual design decision that followed. A qualifying exam is not a puzzle set. Puzzles have a trick; once you know it, you are done, and a model that has read the internet knows most of the tricks. Jobs have deliverables, and deliverables fail in boring ways — the mass budget is 4 kg over, the controller works on the seed you tuned on and nowhere else, the manual you wrote is unreadable by anyone who was not in the room. We wanted a benchmark whose failure modes were those failure modes.

What counted as a task

We started from a list of about forty candidates, drawn from what the people on the team had actually spent time on in the previous two years. Four filters ran over it, and they were unforgiving.

It has to be a deliverable. The agent's output must be a thing — a chassis, a linkage plus a trim program, a policy, a training pipeline, a harness with a manual — not an answer. Anything whose output was a number or a paragraph got cut, because grading a paragraph means grading with a model, and a benchmark that grades with a model inherits that model's blind spots.

It has to be machine-checkable without a human in the loop. Every family is graded by a program that runs unattended, has a wall-clock budget of its own, and produces a number. This is the filter that killed the most candidates and it killed some good ones. "Design a gripper for deformable objects" is real work and we cannot grade it. "Design a mobile base and prove it stable under three different arms" is real work and a physics engine can settle the argument.

It has to admit partial credit. A pass/fail exam of eight items has nine possible scores, which is not enough resolution to rank anything. So every family produces a continuous score, and the continuity has to be honest — not a pass/fail gate with decimals bolted on. Task 08 is the clearest case: settled tower height, overhang past the table edge, weighings used, items surviving the shake. Every one of those is a real number that a better answer moves.

It must not be solvable by recall. If a strong agent could produce a winning submission by remembering a paper, the task measures library coverage rather than engineering. The defence is mostly structural: hidden seeds, blind renderers, held-out tasks, and evaluation conditions the agent is told about but never sees.

The cut

Forty-odd candidates in, eight families out, one retired late in development. The single most common reason for rejection was not difficulty and not scope — it was that we could not write a verifier we trusted more than we trusted the submission.

The eight, and what each one is for

What survived is not a sample of robotics. It is a spread across the axes on which we have seen engineers — human and otherwise — fail differently.

Read down that middle column and the shape of the exam shows up. Two families are design problems with no learning in them at all. Two are learning problems where the interesting constraint is the interface, not the algorithm. One is about writing for another engineer. One removes perception so that reasoning has nowhere to hide. None of them is a benchmark of a single skill, which is deliberate: the job is not a benchmark of a single skill either.

Eight leaderboards, then an index

Each family gets its own ranking, and the rankings are independent. This is not a presentational choice. The families measure different things, on different scales, with different amounts of headroom, and a single number that hides that is a number that lies about it.

The RLE Index is then the unweighted mean of the eight family scores. We tried the alternatives and threw them all away:

  • Weighting by importance. Every weighting we proposed was a claim about what robotics is for, and we could not defend any of them for longer than an afternoon.
  • Weighting by cost or compute. This rewards agents for being expensive on the families that happen to be expensive to run. Cost belongs on its own axis, which is why the site has a cost section rather than a cost-adjusted score.
  • Normalising each family to its observed spread. Tempting, and it makes the numbers look better behaved — but it means a family's contribution to the index changes when a new model is added, and published results should not move because someone else ran the suite.

The unweighted mean has one property that matters more than elegance: you cannot buy the index with one strong dimension. Eight families at equal weight means the ceiling on any single family is 12.5 index points, and the fastest way up is always whichever family you are worst at. That is the incentive we wanted, because it is the incentive a real engineering team has.

The site also offers the index by task rank — the mean of a model's eight placements rather than the mean of its eight scores. The two disagree, and the disagreement is informative. Mean score asks by how much a model wins; mean rank asks how often. A model that is second everywhere and a model that wins three families and trails in five can land in the same place by score and nowhere near each other by rank.

Everything is a budget

Every family carries two clocks: an agent budget and a verifier budget. They are separate on purpose — the verifier's cost is ours, not the agent's, and an agent should never be able to spend its way into a slower grade. Task 03 adds a third: an interaction budget, metered per simulator step, which is the only way that family's efficiency term means anything.

The budgets are also the honest statement of scope. Two hours for a chassis. Three for three lead arms. Eight plus an hour for a kitchen speed-run. Four for a whole-body training pipeline. These are not generous. They were set at roughly what a competent engineer would need with the reference materials open, and then not padded, because a benchmark where everyone finishes measures nothing about prioritisation — and prioritisation under a deadline is most of the job.

What this benchmark does not measure

Worth stating plainly, since a qualifying exam is defined as much by its omissions.

There is no real hardware. Everything is simulated, every physical quantity is a computed one, and sim-to-real is out of scope entirely — task 09 is scored sim-to-sim and says so. There is no human interaction: no clarifying questions, no design review, no stakeholder who changes their mind on day three. There is no long horizon — the longest budget is a working day, and the failure modes of a three-month project are not in here.

And it does not measure novelty. Every family has a reference solution that a person wrote, and the Oracle row on the leaderboard is that solution's score. It is a calibration baseline, not a competitor, and it is deliberately not an upper bound: on at least one split it is beatable, which is the useful thing about publishing it.

The next post goes one level down, into the part of the design that took the most time and shows up least: the verifiers. Every family is graded by a program the agent never sees, and almost every interesting decision in the suite is a decision about what that program is allowed to trust.
