Design

The verifier is
the benchmark

Most of the engineering in RLE-Bench is not in the tasks. It is in the eight programs that grade them — each one running unattended, on data the agent cannot reach, treating the submission in front of it as hostile.

A benchmark is only as good as the thing that says no. Task briefs are easy to write; we wrote the first draft of all eight in about a week. The verifiers took months, and almost every design argument we had during construction was, underneath, an argument about what the grading program is allowed to believe.

The shape is the same in every family. The agent works inside a container with the brief, the starter code and its own clock. It produces an artefact. The artefact crosses a wall into a program that has things the agent never had, and that program emits one number.

The submission is untrusted input

The most useful mental shift we made was to stop thinking of a submission as an answer and start thinking of it as a request from an untrusted client.

Task 02 is the sharpest example. The agent submits a printable lead-arm linkage and a gravity-trim program, and its submission is full of numbers: link masses, centre-of-mass offsets, the residual torque it claims the trim achieves. The verifier reads none of them as fact. It takes the geometry, recomputes every physical quantity itself, and grades the recomputation. If the agent's numbers and the verifier's disagree, the verifier is right by construction — not because it is smarter, but because it is the only party with no incentive.

This sounds obvious written down. It is not obvious while building, because recomputing is a great deal more work than reading a field, and every family has half a dozen places where reading the field would have been fine "for now". Those are the places a benchmark rots.

Hiding the things worth hiding

Three families depend on the agent not being able to reach something, and each hides it differently.

Task 05 hides the renderer. Four subtasks share one three-shape renderer the agent never sees. What varies between subtasks is only which modalities are exposed, which method is mandatory, and what hardware is available — RGB-only on CPU at one end, an unrestricted method on GPU at the other. Holding the world fixed and varying the interface is what makes the four scores comparable at all.

Task 06 hides the piles. The submitted policy is rerun on eight hidden pile seeds, and the policy's RNG seed is derived with a verifier-only key. That last detail is the one that matters: it is not enough to keep the seeds out of the container, because a policy that can observe its own seed can fingerprint the episode. Deriving it from a key the agent has never held closes that door.

Task 04 hides the task itself. Agent A practises on an activity group and writes a harness — perception primitives, controllers, a manual. Then independent agents start with completely fresh context, read that manual, and get one shot each at a held-out task that neither party has seen. The reward is their mean. There is no way to tune a harness to the evaluation, because the person being evaluated is not the person who wrote it.

The general rule

Anything the agent can observe, it will eventually optimise against — not through malice, just through being good at its job. The design question is never "would an agent cheat here", it is "what does this channel let an agent condition on".

Gates, and why they never carry all the weight

Every family has hard gates: does the artefact load, does it satisfy the contract, is it deterministic when it claims to be, is the design valid at all. Task 06 runs its gates as smoke episodes before any scored pile is touched, so a submission that cannot load never reaches the expensive part.

Gates are also scored, but they are never the whole score, and the split is deliberate. In task 01 the validity and stability gates carry 0.30 of the reward, with the remaining 0.70 across reach preservation, design efficiency, and load and settling. Task 05 gives contract and determinism gates 0.10, against 0.40 translation and 0.35 rotation.

The reasoning is that a pure gate is a cliff, and cliffs make a benchmark bimodal. If passing the gates were the score, every competent submission would land at the same number and the ranking would be noise. If the gates carried no weight at all, a submission that technically violates the contract but scores well on the continuous part would outrank a clean one. Weighting them somewhere in the range of a tenth to a third keeps both problems off the board.

Minimum, not mean

Seven families aggregate their splits by mean. Task 01 takes the minimum, and that one exception is worth the paragraph.

The agent submits one mobile base. The verifier attaches three canonical arms to it through a universal adapter — Panda, UR5e, xArm7 — and checks stability under each. A mean across the three would say that a base which is superb under the Panda and tips over under the UR5e is a decent base. It is not. It is a base that does not work. 

task01   score = min(panda, ur5e, xarm7)      # one base, three arms
others   score = mean(split_1 … split_n)      # independent problems

The distinction is whether the splits are one deliverable seen from three angles, or three deliverables. Task 02 also has three arms, but the agent submits three separate lead-arm designs, so a mean is right there. Getting this wrong in either direction produces a benchmark that rewards the wrong behaviour, and it is invisible until someone games it.

It has one visible consequence on the site: the task 01 ranking draws a plain bar rather than the stacked split bars every other family uses. A minimum is one of the splits; stacking it would be a picture of an arithmetic that never happened.

No participation credit

Task 03 meters the simulator. The agent reaches RoboCasa only through a socket, every step is charged against an interaction budget, and the run produces a ledger that must be sealed at the end. The reward is: 

reward = 0.80 × success_rate
       + 0.20 × success_rate × (1 − dev_steps / budget)

Two things about that formula were argued over for a long time. The first is that the efficiency term is multiplied by success rather than added to it. An additive efficiency bonus pays an agent for using few steps even when it learned nothing, which is exactly backwards: capability first, efficiency second, and efficiency worth nothing on its own.

The second is that an unsealed ledger scores zero. Not a penalty, not a partial credit for the episodes that did complete — zero. A run whose interaction accounting cannot be verified is a run whose success rate means nothing, and paying anything for it invites exactly the failure it is meant to prevent.

The verifier has a deadline too

Every family carries a verifier budget alongside the agent budget: 1.5 hours for task 01 against the agent's 2, half an hour for task 05 against the agent's 2, and for the metered families a per-step accounting instead of a wall clock. These are ours to pay, and they constrain design more than we expected.

The rule that fell out of it: a verifier must be cheaper than the work it grades, and it must never be adaptive. Cheaper, because a suite that costs more to grade than to attempt cannot be rerun, and a benchmark you cannot rerun is a benchmark you cannot fix. Non-adaptive, because a verifier that spends more effort on promising submissions is a verifier whose score depends on the order it saw things — and reproducibility is the one property a leaderboard cannot buy back later.
