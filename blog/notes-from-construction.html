Findings

Notes from
construction

The things we did not know when we started, in the order we found them out. Most of them changed the suite; one of them left a hole in the numbering that we decided to keep.

Building a benchmark is mostly discovering that your design was answering a slightly different question than the one you meant to ask. What follows is the list of those discoveries — the ones that survived into the shipped suite, with the reasoning intact rather than tidied away.

The harness turned out to be the variable

Task 03 started as one task: learn a RoboCasa kitchen subtask through a metered socket. The three harness levels were an implementation detail — we needed a way to make the task tractable for weaker agents, so we built a ladder.

It became the most interesting thing in the family. Because the task, the seeds and the reward weights are identical at every level, and the only thing that changes is what the agent is handed, the gap between an L1 score and an L3 score on the same subtask is a clean measurement of one quantity: what the harness bought.

Once we had that, two other design decisions fell out almost for free. Task 08 ships only L3 — the full skill library, privileged poses and perception service — because its five tabletop problems are about physical reasoning, and shipping anything less would mean grading perception under the name of reasoning. And task 04 inverts the whole thing: instead of measuring what a harness buys, it asks the agent to build one, then measures what it bought for somebody else.

The lesson we would generalise

If you find yourself building scaffolding so that weaker agents can attempt your task, stop and check whether the scaffolding is the experiment. It usually is, and holding everything else fixed across it costs almost nothing.

Writing for a stranger is a different skill

Task 04 is the family we were least sure about and are now most attached to. Agent A practises on an activity group and writes a harness — perception primitives, controllers, a manual. Independent agents then start with fresh context, read only that manual, and get one shot each at a held-out task neither has seen. Agent A's score is their mean.

What this exposes, and nothing else in the suite does, is the gap between code that works and code that transfers. An agent that solves its practice group brilliantly and documents it as a stream of consciousness scores badly, because the readers cannot use it. An agent that builds three clean primitives and writes two pages of honest limitations scores well. That ordering is the whole point, and it is not a property any single-agent task can measure.

It is also the most expensive family to run and the most annoying to debug, because a bad score has two possible causes and they look identical from the outside: the harness was bad, or the readers were. Averaging over five independent readers is the blunt instrument we settled on.

A benchmark needs a floor nobody has cleared

Task 04's difficulty bands are EASY, MEDIUM and HARD, five activity groups each. The HARD band is doors and enclosed cavities, and it is close to unsolved.

The obvious move is to cut it or soften it. We kept it, and the reasoning is the one thing on this page we would defend hardest. A benchmark whose hardest band is being cleared has already stopped being useful, and you find out about it a year late, once the top of the leaderboard has compressed into noise. A band that is known to be uncleared today is the only part of the suite guaranteed to still have headroom next year.

It costs something: the HARD scores contribute very little ranking signal right now, and they drag the family mean down uniformly, which makes task 04 look harder than its EASY and MEDIUM bands alone would suggest. That is a fair description of the task, so we let it stand.

Retiring a task is cheaper than renumbering one

There is no task07. The slot existed, was built, and was removed during development — and we left the numbering alone rather than closing the gap.

This is a small decision that we expect to be quietly grateful for. Renumbering would have made every existing result, log directory, internal document and half-written script point at the wrong task, and the error would be silent: task08 would still resolve, just to something else. A gap in a sequence is a question someone asks once and gets answered. A shifted sequence is a class of bug that never fully goes away.

Identifiers are addresses, not an ordering. Once one has been published, it has been published.

The same instinct is why the family IDs on the site are stable deep links — #task03 or ?task=task03 goes straight to a family's ranking, and will keep going there.

Calibration is slower than everything else

Task 09 asks for a whole-body tracking pipeline for a Unitree G1, scored against five retargeted LAFAN1 clips: dance, fight, fall and get up, run, sprint. Each motion needs its own thresholds, because "good tracking" on a 20-second sprint is not the same tracking error as on a fall-and-recover.

Today only the dance thresholds are calibrated. The other four copy them, carrying a calibrated = false flag in the manifest.

We considered hiding this — copied thresholds still produce a number, and the number looks like all the other numbers. Shipping the flag instead was the right call for a reason that generalises past this task: an uncalibrated threshold is not a wrong result, it is a result with unknown units. A reader who knows the sprint bar was inherited from the dance bar can weigh a sprint score correctly. A reader who does not, cannot, and will not know to ask.

The broader finding is just that calibration is the long pole. Writing a task takes days. Writing its verifier takes weeks. Working out what score a competent human actually gets, so the scale means anything, takes longer than both and cannot be parallelised, because it is mostly one person running one reference solution and staring at the trace.

Continuous scores, and the Oracle you can beat

Two smaller things, from the same instinct.

Every family that could be pass/fail is not. Task 08 scores settled tower height, overhang past the edge, weighings used, and items surviving the shake — all continuous, because the difference between a tower that falls immediately and one that stands for four seconds is real information that a boolean throws away.

And the Oracle reference — the human-written solution for each family — is published on the leaderboard as a row that is explicitly not ranked. It is a calibration baseline, and it is deliberately not an upper bound. It was written under roughly the same budget as the agents get, by someone who knew the task, and on some splits it is beatable. A ceiling that turns out to be reachable is much more useful than a ceiling asserted to be unreachable, and rather more honest about how the reference was produced.

What we would do differently

Three, briefly, since a construction post that ends on satisfaction is not a construction post.

  • Build the verifier first. Every family where we wrote the brief first had to have the brief rewritten once the verifier existed. The families where we started from "what can we prove" needed no such round trip.
  • Budget the calibration explicitly. We planned time for eight tasks and eight verifiers. We did not plan time for eight calibrations, and task 09 is still carrying the consequences in a manifest flag.
  • Decide the aggregation rule before writing the splits. Whether splits are one deliverable seen three ways or three separate deliverables determines min versus mean, and it also determines how the task should be written. Task 01 is a minimum because it is one base; realising that late meant reworking the brief so it read like one deliverable.

The suite is v1.0-dev and the leaderboard is still showing placeholder scores. When real runs land, the numbers on the front page will change and this post will not — these are the design findings, and they were true before any model was scored against them.

Budgets, split structure, band definitions and the manifest flags described here are read from the benchmark repo. No per-model scores appear in this post — the leaderboard data is placeholder until real runs land.
